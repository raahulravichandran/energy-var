The honest headline

The calculation is easy; the two hours that make it credible are the data cleaning and the backtest. Both changed the story. Three findings survive scrutiny, and two of them invert the pre-registered guess:

  • Data hygiene decided the gas result before any model did. The free continuous NG=F series is unadjusted, so it manufactures a fake return every time the front-month rolls. Fourteen of 36 roll days move more than ±10%; one is a spurious −64% log splice (−47.5% in simple terms). Cleaning the roll days cut gas excess kurtosis from 35 to 7. WTI rolls were immaterial on the CME crude calendar (1 of 35), and oil’s fat tails are real.
  • The normal assumption understates the tail for oil, not gas — the reverse of the guess. At 99% the empirical VaR runs 1.27–1.28× the parametric number for Brent and WTI; for gas the normal is, if anything, conservative.
  • √t scaling to 10 days is not rejected. Every variance ratio straddles 1 (Lo–MacKinlay p > 0.58). Three years simply isn't enough to catch the vol-clustering effect at this horizon; the point estimates even lean the other way (mild mean reversion in oil).

The Kupiec backtest ties it together, and a fourth point emerges from it: the binding scaling risk in this book isn't the √t convention — it's procyclicality. A VaR calibrated on the calm 2023–24 tape was simply overrun when the crude complex sold off in spring 2026.

01

The catch: a continuous-contract artifact

Futures risk studies live or die on the return series. yfinance's BZ=F / CL=F / NG=F / HO=F are unadjusted continuous front-months: on the day the front contract rolls, the series splices two different contracts and books the gap as a "return."

Henry Hub's term structure is steep and seasonal — winter contracts trade far above shoulder-season ones — so the splice is large and systematic. Identifying roll days by the CME rule (last trade three business days before the delivery month; the continuous series rolls the next session) and measuring the return on each shows the damage: 14 of 36 gas roll days exceed ±10%, topped by a −64% log move (−47.5% simple) on 29 Jan 2026 that is entirely the February→March roll off a cold-snap spike.

Treatment, applied consistently rather than to taste: drop roll-day returns for gas; leave oil raw. Oil earns the reprieve on evidence — its largest moves (Brent −16%, WTI −18%, heating oil −16%) cluster on ordinary mid-month dates in March–April 2026 and move together, the signature of a real crude selloff, and its worst roll day is a trivial 11%. Gas carries both versions through the rest of this note so nothing is hidden.

03

Portfolio VaR & the diversification it buys

Equal weights (25% each), portfolio variance w′Σw, compared against the sum of standalone VaRs. This is the step that turns four calculations into a portfolio.

At 99% the equal-weight book carries a 6.6% daily historical VaR (5.3% parametric). Added as if perfectly correlated, the standalone VaRs sum to 6.9% on the same parametric basis. Against the 5.3% parametric portfolio VaR that gap is a 23.8% diversification benefit — and it is almost entirely a gas story.

The correlation matrix shows why. Brent, WTI and heating oil sit at 0.80–0.94 — for risk purposes they are one position in three tickers. Gas correlates just 0.14 with crude. It is the only genuine diversifier in an energy book, and it earns that role by being the wildest name in it.

04

Does the model actually work? Kupiec POF

A VaR number is a hypothesis, and the backtest is the test. Estimating VaR on a rolling 250-day window, stepping forward out-of-sample and counting exceptions, a 99% model should breach ~1% of days. The Kupiec proportion-of-failures test is the likelihood-ratio on that breach count.

The 95% column carries the sting in the tail. Even historical 95% VaR is rejected for Brent and heating oil (breach ~7%). A rolling window that spent 2023–24 learning a calm market set a threshold the April-2026 selloff blew straight through. That is procyclicality, and at 95% it does more damage than the choice of distribution.

05

Finding I — the normal misleads on oil, not gas

Prediction reversed

The guess was that the normal assumption would understate worst in gas. After cleaning, it understates in the oil complex and is conservative for gas.

At 99%, historical-over-parametric VaR runs 1.27× (Brent), 1.28× (WTI) and 1.04× (heating oil) — the normal quantile sits inside the empirical one, exactly the fat-tail understatement the backtest then convicts. Gas comes in at 0.89×: the normal is over-stated there.

The reason is instructive rather than embarrassing. Once the roll splices are gone, cleaned gas has enormous variance (66% p.a.) but only mildly fat, near-symmetric tails at the 1% quantile — its excess mass lives further out, in the 99.9% region and in ES, not at the 99% VaR point. A normal calibrated to that large σ therefore places its 2.33σ quantile beyond the empirical one. Left in raw form, gas would have shown a 0.77× ratio — the artifact inflating σ and making the normal look even more conservative. The lesson survives either way: the normal assumption fails where you can least afford it — in the liquid, "well-behaved" oil names — and the failure is confirmed out-of-sample, not just in a moment estimate.

06

Finding II — √t scaling holds, within the error bars

Prediction reversed

The guess was that volatility clustering would make actual 10-day risk exceed √10 × 1-day. In this sample the scaling is not rejected — and the point estimates lean the other way.

Comparing √10 × (1-day 99% VaR) against the actual 10-day distribution gives ratios of 0.82–0.95×: scaling nominally over-states multi-day risk here. But the honest test is the variance ratio with its sampling error. The heteroskedasticity-robust Lo–MacKinlay statistic returns VR(10) of 0.95, 0.90, 1.08 and 0.98 — and none is distinguishable from 1 (p = 0.78, 0.59, 0.70, 0.93).

So the defensible statement is a negative one, and it matters: three years of daily data cannot reject √t at the 10-day horizon. The vol-clustering critique of the Basel scaling convention is real in principle, but it needs either a longer sample or a GARCH/filtered-historical treatment to demonstrate — a naive empirical comparison here would have "found" an effect that the standard errors don't support. The scaling risk that is visible in this data is the procyclical one from §04, not the horizon convention.

07

Caveats & what the defensible next step is

  • Continuous-contract returns, not a traded P&L. Even roll-cleaned, these are front-month price returns; a real book rolls at the calendar spread and holds a weighted strip. Back-adjusted (Panama) or per-contract series would refine the gas tail further.
  • Three years spans one vol regime shift, not many. It is enough to convict the normal at 99% and to expose procyclicality; it is not enough to pin down 10-day scaling or the 99.9% tail. Treat every number as conditioned on the 2023–26 tape.
  • Equal weights are a stand-in. The diversification figure is real but weight-dependent; a desk would run it on live book weights.
  • The upgrade path is filtered historical simulation — an EWMA/GARCH vol filter under the historical quantile. It addresses the two real weaknesses here at once: it makes the tail respond to the current regime (the §04 procyclicality) and it produces horizon scaling that doesn't lean on √t.
